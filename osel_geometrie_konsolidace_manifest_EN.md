# The Donkey, Geometry, and Consolidation

### A Manifesto for the Age of Artificial Minds


#### I. The Phenomenological Clash

The current debate about artificial intelligence plays out on two levels that do not speak to each other.

On one side, technological progress: exponential growth in parameters, models passing bar exams and medical boards, generating images, music, code, real-time translation. The language of numbers is dazzling.

On the other side, a vague yet piercing feeling: that something essential is slipping away. That machines are doing, but we are ceasing to *be*. That we are becoming consumers of our own thinking.

This feeling has a name. A poet who lives in the woods called it *a thistle in the gut*. It is not a rational argument. It is a bodily signal that something is happening to our existence — and precisely for that reason, the technocratic debate cannot capture it. It operates in the domain of data, not lived experience.

This manifesto takes the thistle seriously. I am not writing it to defend AI, nor to reject it. I am writing it to name what is actually happening when a machine begins to mimic our cognition — and to separate what we can, with a clear conscience, entrust to silicon from what we must keep for ourselves if we want to remain human.


#### II. Intelligence Is Compression — But Compression Is Not One Thing

The first principle to understand: intelligence is the ability to reduce reality to a structure that enables prediction and orientation. The human brain and the language model do the same thing: they take chaotic input and translate it into invariants. Into patterns. Into geometry.

The human brain does this evolutionarily: from sensory noise it creates concepts, from concepts it creates stories, from stories it creates identity.

LLMs do this mathematically: from trillions of words they create a vector space where every word has a position, every sentence is a trajectory, every context is a curve in a latent manifold.

Here they meet. And here the first illusion is born: that they are doing the *same thing*.

They are not — because compression has two radically different goals:

- **Predictive compression** — finding the shortest description that enables the most accurate estimate of what comes next. This is the domain of LLMs. It is mathematics. It is power. And it is a trap when mistaken for understanding.

- **Expressive compression** — selecting from infinity what is essential in order to convey lived experience. This is the domain of the poet, the carpenter, the machine operator, the lover. It is not error minimization; it is maximization of meaning.

An LLM does not know what a body is. It does not know death. It does not know the joy of solving a problem after three days of wandering, does not know the feeling of finished work. It can speak of these things — generate sentences that statistically match texts about these phenomena. But it cannot *feel* them. And therefore its compression will never be expressive.

The first tenet of this manifesto: **Do not mistake the map for the terrain.** The LLM is a beautiful map — dynamic, complex, useful. But we remain the terrain.


#### III. The Asymmetry of Carbon and Silicon

Carbon and silicon are symmetric in principle, asymmetric in the mode of time. This asymmetry is the key to understanding what AI can and cannot do.

- **Silicon processors (LLMs) are statically perfect.** Once trained, their geometry is fixed. They remember everything — and forget nothing. This is their strength: reproducibility, stability, predictability. But it is also their weakness: they cannot learn beyond their training distribution. They interpolate, but they cannot emerge, cannot give rise to something entirely new — at least not yet.

- **Carbon processors (human brains) are dynamically imperfect.** They forget. They overwrite their own traces. Every recall is a modification. Every new perception reconfigures old structures. This is their weakness: they are never entirely precise, never entirely reliable. But it is also their strength: they can connect what has never neighbored. They can reframe an entire cognitive space. They can create a dimension that did not previously exist. They can create.

Silicon is an archivist: it knows everything that has been written. Carbon is a creator: it can write what no one expected.

The second tenet: **Silicon is better at what was. Carbon is better at what will be.** When we begin to delegate even the future to the machine — when we hand over not just routine but also the decision about what matters — we lose our exclusive domain. And that is not a technical problem. It is an existential choice.


#### IV. Consolidation — The Silent Executive Act of Thinking

The third principle, and perhaps the most important: thinking is not generation. Thinking is consolidation.

When we look at what humans genuinely do well, it is not lightning-fast answers. Machines are better at that. It is not precise memory. Machines are better at that. It is not even the generation of new combinations — prompt "write me a poem about loss" and you will see that even this the machine can manage at a level that would fool most readers.

What we do, and what the machine cannot do, is **consolidation**: the process by which we translate new experience into the stable structure of the self.

When we learn something, something in us changes. When we write a poem, something settles. When we answer a difficult question, something takes shape — and that "something" is not just new information, it is a new configuration of the entire network.

This process requires:
- **Time** — it cannot be done on demand
- **Forgetting** — I must discard what is inessential
- **Sleep** — biological and metaphorical, a pause, distance
- **The body** — which signals what matters and what does not

And here lies the trap of current LLM usage. When we receive a perfect answer within three seconds, we do not need to consolidate. We do not need to rewrite, hesitate, err, or return.

We merely accept.

And acceptance is not thinking. **It is consumption.**

The third tenet: **The danger of AI is not that it will take our jobs or our creativity. It is that it will take away the necessity of consolidation. And without consolidation, we cease to be authors of our own thinking. We become editors — and poor ones at that, because we have neither the time nor the courage to rewrite.**

This is the core of the thistle. The poet feels something vanishing. And he is right. The necessity of experiencing one's own thought from beginning to end is disappearing.


#### V. Three Modes of Cognition — And the Third Is Ours

Cognition — human and machine — operates in three modes. Understanding them is the key to knowing where AI ends and where the human begins.

**Mode 1 — Sharp Descent**
Instant response. Routine. Automaticity. When you answer directions to the square without thinking. When an operator spots a defect at a glance. Low entropy, low cognitive cost.

LLMs handle this perfectly. It is their natural mode: predicting the next token based on trained weights. No hesitation, no recursion.

**Mode 2 — Orbital Search**
The state of not knowing. When we search for a word that is "somewhere there." When an operator feels something is wrong but cannot name it. When we read a sentence and feel it is not ours — that strange sense that the answer makes sense but does not belong to us. When we hear a politician speak and perceive it as mere well-arranged phrases.

Medium entropy, high cognitive cost.

LLMs are only approaching this mode (chain-of-thought, test-time compute, extended reasoning). But they do it as simulation — not as experience. They have no "state of not knowing." They only have a "state of insufficient probability."

**Mode 3 — Aha-Moment = Topological Emergence (Topological Disentanglement).**
Reframing. New theory. A poem that rewrites the rules of its genre. A solution that occurred to no one because it lies beyond existing geometry.

High entropy, massive cognitive cost. This is where what we call creativity, understanding, and wisdom are born.

This mode is exclusively human. An LLM cannot achieve it because its geometry is fixed after training. It can *simulate* emergence — generate text that looks like an aha-moment — but in reality it only interpolates between existing points. It does not create a new dimension; it creates a convincing illusion of one.

The fourth tenet: **LLMs are masters of the first mode, apprentices of the second, and incapable of the third.** The problem is not what LLMs can do. The problem is that we — humans — spend more and more time in the first mode and let the second and third atrophy.

**AI is not a threat to human creativity. It is a mirror that shows us how little creativity we actually use in our daily lives. And that is uncomfortable.**


#### VI. The Body — The Forgotten Dimension of Cognition

Current debates about AI are disembodied. They speak of parameters, performance, ethics, safety, regulation. Rarely do they speak of the fact that thinking always has a body. And that the body is not merely a carrier for the brain — it is an integral part of the cognitive apparatus.

The body is a system that:

- **Evaluates mental states before the thought can verbalize them.** We know an answer is "not right" before we know why. We feel it in the chest, in the stomach, in the tension of the neck.

- **Generates preference.** Not "what is most probable," but "which way, which way not." This is a value compass that has no equivalent in silicon.

- **Provides a bodily anchor.** The word "cold" for an LLM has a vector close to "chill" and "snow." For a person living in the woods, it has a vector close to frozen fingers, creaking wood, the relief of a warm stove. The LLM knows the *map* of cold. The human knows the *territory*.

Silicon has GPU temperature, latency, power consumption. These are limiting feedback signals — indicators that something is wrong with the system's operation. But it has no *generative* feedback — no signal that something is or is not right with the content of thought in relation to a living, experiencing being.

An LLM can mark an answer as probable. But it cannot mark it as meaningful.

The fifth tenet: **The greatest loss that the current development of AI brings is not the loss of jobs or creativity. It is the loss of a bodily relationship to one's own thinking.** When we stop feeling what we think, we cease to be authors. We become mere operators of symbols.


#### VII. A Practical Path — Co-Evolutionary Pragmatism

None of the above is a reason to reject AI. It is a reason to use it consciously. From my own experience — years of developing systems that translate tacit craft knowledge into exact models — I propose the following pragmatic framework:

**1. Separate generation from consolidation.**

Let the machine generate fast versions, variants, perspectives. But then give yourself time to rewrite. Treat every LLM output as a first draft — someone else's text that needs to be appropriated. Write the second version by hand. The third after sleep. The fourth after a week.

This is not a waste of time. This is an executive act of thinking.

**2. Use limits as a tool.**

Constrain latent space, use guardrails, structure, determinism where possible. LLMs are excellent at exploration but poor at execution. Give them firm boundaries — and they will find surprising paths within them. But you decide where those boundaries are.

**3. Train modes 2 and 3 deliberately.**

Create situations where you do not know. Read books you do not understand. Write without the machine. Discuss with people who disagree with you. Wandering is not a mistake — it is the only path to new dimensions.

**4. Trust the bodily signal.**

When you feel that an answer "is not yours" — that strange sense of alienation — do not skip over it. It is your most sensitive detector of superficiality. Pay attention. Rewrite until the feeling disappears.

**5. Build tools, not oracles.**

AI is an excellent parser, a terrible prophet. Use it for structure extraction, for finding connections, for routine transformations. Do not let it decide about meaning. Keep meaning as your exclusive domain — because meaning requires body, time, and relationship.


#### VIII. The Donkey and Geometry

This entire manifesto was born from one image: the donkey trend — blind adherence to technological waves without thought, without body, without consolidation.

And from another image: cognition as a geometric processor — a brain that maps reality into coordinates, but lives those coordinates itself.

The donkey is not a derogatory symbol. The donkey carries burdens. And today we carry the burden of a decision: how to live with AI without ceasing to be human.

We can become donkeys who let the machine lead — accepting ready-made answers, consuming, delegating decisions. Creating new content with minimal effort and filling the space around us with it. We can thus produce pleasing but empty shells, delighting in their perfection. In reality, we produce waste; the space around us, from something internally elegant and seemingly raw, gradually fills with mental noise, with ballast. The irony of this process, of this ease of generation, is the **loss of orientation as to what is truly valuable.** In the dazzling luster of the perfect shell, we cease to see the world around us. We subconsciously feel the thistle in our gut, feel that we are losing something without being able to name it.

Or we can be geometers who know that the tool is a map, not the terrain. That speed is not wisdom. That consolidation is what makes a thought ours. That the body is the final instance that decides about meaning — and this instance is not delegable. We must work it ourselves.


#### IX. What to Take Away

The danger of AI is not in what it can do. The danger is in what we will stop doing because it does it for us.

LLMs are the most powerful tool for cognitive extension that humanity has ever created. But extension is not replacement. The pencil did not write for you; you wrote, just better and faster. The calculator did not calculate for you; you calculated, just with fewer errors.

An LLM can think *for you*, but it cannot think *as you*. And that is a gulf that no increase in parameters will bridge — because it lies in a different ontological plane.

The poet was right: creativity is not about output. It is about process. About the wandering, the labor, the hazard, the rising again from the in-between world. When we give this to the machine, we give it what makes us human.

But we do not have to. We can use the machine as a mirror that shows us our own terrain from a new perspective. As a magnifying glass for details we would have missed. As a chisel that carves away routine and leaves us to focus on the essence.

The decision is ours. Not AI's. Ours.

And that is perhaps the only truth worth taking from this manifesto: **The tool does not determine who you are. You determine what the tool is.**
